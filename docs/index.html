Personalized speech recognition · German

Personal Voice Control for atypical speech using LoRA

Speech recognition that learns one person's voice from 12 minutes of recordings, runs offline on a laptop, and never acts on a command it is unsure of.

Language: GermanSpeakers so far: 1 (anonymized)Runs on: Apple laptop, offlineLive demo: home control and booking dialogs

TL;DR

How well it works

  • Standard German speech recognition got 55.4% of our first user's words wrong.
  • With 12 minutes of his recordings, a personal adapter and a sound-based correction step bring that to 10.7%: about 5× fewer errors.
  • 96% of characters are right (4.0% character error rate).
  • 0 wrong actions in a replay of 15 commands: when unsure, it asked.

Why this is the right approach

  • About 100× less data for the same result. The closest published study needed 22.5 hours of one speaker's speech to reach 10.7%, and had 15.8% with 1.4 hours [1]. We reach 10.7% with 12 minutes. (Different speaker and test set; an equal-footing test is next.)
  • Personal, not generic. One person's adapter makes others worse (60.6% → 109.4%), so the product is a fast way to make each person their own model.
  • The errors left are near misses (13.2% word vs 4.3% character errors), exactly what sound-based matching fixes.
  • Safe by design: when unsure it offers 2–3 choices, so a mistake costs one tap, not a wrong action.

How it scales

  • Short onboarding: ~350 short prompts (~12 min of speech), then a scripted pipeline trains, compresses and installs a 0.57 GB personal model.
  • Private and cheap to run: daily recognition runs offline on an ordinary laptop, with no cloud service.
  • Gets better with use (next): each choice the user makes becomes training data.
  • Less recording per new user (planned): a shared starting model plus a small personal adapter.
  • Beyond Whisper: a wav2vec2 version is in progress. It cannot loop or invent fluent text, and it can score each allowed command directly.

Why phonemic repair

The gap between WER and CER pointed us to sound-based fixes

After adaptation, errors are small

With the LoRA, WER is 13.2% but CER only 4.3%, three times lower. Most wrong words are wrong by a letter or two: geklabt for geklappt, Seben for Sieben. The model heard the sounds; it picked the wrong spelling or a non-word. A near-miss like that costs a full word in WER.

That kind of error is what a phonetic code catches. Kölner Phonetik gives near-miss words the same code, and the weighted edit distance prefers the confusions dysarthria causes (voicing, dropped h, doubled consonants). Snap lowers WER from 13.2% to 10.7% while CER barely moves (4.3% → 4.0%), because it fixes one or two letters in words that were almost right.

Before adaptation, errors are too large

On the base model CER is 24.4%: whole words are wrong, not single letters. Snap has little to work with there (WER 55.4% → 53.7%; CER even rises slightly to 24.8%), because the wrong words are too far from the right ones in sound.

So the order matters: the LoRA adapter brings the acoustic output close, and then sound-based repair can finish the job. This is also why we measure both: WER tells the user's experience, CER tells us what kind of error remains and which fix fits.

Key finding: adaptation is personal

The adapter cuts his error rate by 80%, but on other dysarthric speakers it nearly doubles the error (60.6% → 109.4%; about 108% over all 1272 clips). Dysarthria differs a lot between people, so a model tuned to one voice learns that voice. The method works, and scaling it means an onboarding process that produces one small adapter per person.

Limits

What these results do and do not show

Limits

  • One speaker. The test set is 39 sentences, so one sentence moves WER by several points.
  • The snap rules were designed while looking at dev errors. The German sound-variant rules improved dev but not test, a sign of overfitting.
  • The external German dysarthric data has no license or source information. We use it for internal comparison only.
  • Augmentation, synthetic speech and DoRA are built but not yet evaluated.

Privacy by design

  • Audio and transcripts never leave local or lab machines and are never committed to the code repository.
  • Inference runs offline on the user's own laptop.
  • No cloud speech APIs on patient audio unless consent covers it explicitly.

Proposed · learning loop

Teaching the model through daily use

When the system is unsure it already asks "Meinten Sie …?" and offers two or three options. Each time he picks one, he gives us something valuable: the correct text for that recording, and the wrong texts the model found plausible. Today that information is thrown away. We propose to keep it (locally, with consent) and learn from it in three stages, from simplest to most ambitious.

StageHow it learnsWhat it fixes
1 · Confirmed labelsSave each picked option with its audio as a new training pair. Retrain the adapter every week or two, the same way as onboarding.Adds real everyday speech (tired days, noisy rooms, new words) without extra recording sessions. The most reliable first step.
2 · Preference learning (the reinforcement-learning part)For the same audio, the picked option is "preferred" and the rejected ones are "dispreferred". Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) trains the adapter to raise the preferred text and lower the others, with no separate reward model. A policy-gradient variant uses "picked" as the reward.The rejected options are exactly the confusions this person's speech causes (e.g. voiced vs voiceless consonants), so the model unlearns them directly.
3 · Tuning the rulesUse the picks to update the phonetic confusion costs in snap and the confidence threshold for asking, per person.Fewer confirmation questions over time, without retraining the neural model.

Safeguards

  • Always offer "none of these", so he is never forced to pick a wrong option.
  • Train only on choices he made, never on answers the system accepted by itself, so errors do not feed back into the model.
  • The held-out test set never changes. A new adapter replaces the old one only if test WER does not get worse.
  • People sometimes pick a "close enough" option to save effort. Review a sample of picks with a carer or therapist.
  • Storing daily-use audio needs its own consent, and it stays on the device or lab machine.

Why it matters for scaling

If daily use keeps improving the model, a new person could start with 2–5 minutes of recordings instead of 12 and let the system learn the rest. Onboarding then becomes shorter and cheaper. The research question: how many picks equal one minute of scripted recording?

StepWhat and why
